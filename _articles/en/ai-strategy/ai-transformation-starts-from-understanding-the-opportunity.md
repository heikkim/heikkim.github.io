## AI transformation requires deliberation

Almost half of European SMEs already use AI. The European Commission asked them how. The answer was *"mostly in a limited and experimental way"*. ([Eurobarometer](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/growing-use-ai-european-companies-2026-10-14_en), October 2026)

Limited and experimental is what it looks like from the inside. There is a licence on a few laptops, a trial that has not been extended, and a colleague in the sales team who is quietly using AI to write first drafts. **Everyone agrees that AI matters.** Not that many have been asked what should change as a result.

This is not wasted effort! The people who use the tools get good at them, and what they learn stays with them. What the company has so far is a set of improvements that belongs to whoever holds the licence. The way past that is a decision about what should change, and that decision belongs to the leadership team.

> Every team solves the problem in front of them, so licenses spread and experiments multiply, each reasonable on its own, but fails to create AI utilisation benefits at scale.

AI transformation reaches well beyond deploying AI tools. It is what happens when the way your company works changes because of what AI makes possible, most importantly:
<ul class="success-list">
  <li>Decisions made with better information.</li>
  <li>Hours freed from low value adding manual work.</li>
  <li>Focus is back on the work only people can shine in.</li>
</ul>

That transformation begins with an answer to two relatively narrow questions. This is something you as a company should spend time on in order to create a lasting change initiative.

## Two things to understand, in order

The first one is to find out what AI genuinely does **today** that is relevant to our business? It's a target that keeps moving. The field moves faster than companies can follow it, and since you last checked, something has already shifted. Knowing what the tools do now is what leads you to where they could help.

The second is to understand where in your business AI would change the outcome? That one comes from inside the company, and it comes from the way your work actually gets done, such as where the time goes, where a handoff breaks, and where somebody corrects what somebody else already did. The technology is the same everywhere. Your work is where it gets specific.

An opportunity is a specific place in that work, such as a task someone repeats often, with a visible cost when it goes slowly or wrong. Sometimes the answer to the problem is AI, and sometimes it is not. Oftentimes a whole work process should be rethought.

## A solid plan makes tool choice obvious

When you know what the work needs to become, the tool choice takes care of itself. 

Contra this with starting from the tools instead. Something predictable happens. Every team solves the problem in front of them, so licenses spread and experiments multiply, each reasonable on its own, but fails to create AI utilisation benefits at scale.

More often than not, the problem is ownership. In a July 2026 survey of 297 CHROs, *"78% of leaders have no alignment on where AI creates value"* ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-10-26-gartner-hr-research-finds-77-percent-of-board-members-expect-ai-to-deliver-productivity-and-cost-savings-yet-just-3-percent-of-organizations-exceed-cost-savings-goals-via-productivity)). With every team building its own version of AI use, the company ends up busy with tools in every corner, and it becomes hard to say what it gained.

To succeed, a leader who is accountable for the results and who understands the big picture is utmost necessary. Otherwise your AI transformation will run slow, or stifle out.

## Goals the whole company can act on

AI runs inside the company, as one of the ways it reaches goals it already has, such as topline growth, better margin, a market experiment, or faster delivery. Having solid goals is as relevant as it ever was.

Whatever the pursuit, the leadership team needs to be clear which one AI is pointed at. Generic goals make that hard. They sound like ambition and leave the team short of anything to work with. A useful goal describes a change in the business. Technology matters, and it has a place in the goal, but the goal itself lives in the business. Consider the following goals:

<ul class="common-list">
<li class="li-fail"><strong>Weak.</strong> Improve company automation level with AI.</li>
<li class="li-success"><strong>Clearer.</strong> Every new salesperson has a usable first draft of a customer proposal in their first week, and senior colleagues stay with their clients.</li>
<li class="li-success"><strong>Impactful.</strong> The finance team stops reconciling invoices by hand, and we can see the hours it gave back.</li>
</ul>

What the last two have in common is that you can point at them at the end of the month and say yes or no. The goal names the outcome, so the team acts on it with the same understanding you had.

Setting priorities at the leadership level stops the organisation working on its own problem. Priorities set once and understood everywhere beat every team working in its own silo.

## Opportunity first, then let the tools follow

The order matters more than the speed. An answer to what AI is capable of **today** first, then a goal, then a tool, then the skills to use it, with useful experimentation running alongside the whole way.
