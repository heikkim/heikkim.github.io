## AI potential is a set of use cases

Ninety-seven percent of organisations cannot reach their cost savings goals through productivity alone. ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-10-26-gartner-hr-research-finds-77-percent-of-board-members-expect-ai-to-deliver-productivity-and-cost-savings-yet-just-3-percent-of-organizations-exceed-cost-savings-goals-via-productivity), October 2026)

AI is very good at productivity. The savings land when the work around it changes, and that starts with naming one place in your business where the change would matter.

AI potential is concrete. In practice it is a set of use cases where value can be uncovered. A use case is specific. It is a piece of work that happens often enough to matter, where a person can tell whether the output is good, and where doing it faster or better changes something a customer or the business notices.

The distance between a use case and a wish is easy to see. "We should use AI more" is a wish. "The weekly customer report takes six hours to assemble and the numbers come from four systems" is a use case. It has a frequency, an owner and an outcome.

Finding the candidates takes method rather than inspiration. The effort lives or dies in what happens after the list exists. Three things decide it. The list stops belonging to anyone, the process gets automated when it should have been redesigned, and the first use case dies quietly, because it was too big to learn from, because nobody owned what came out of it, or because the team was never really behind it.

## Finding the candidates in five steps

Start with the work itself. Pick a process that runs every week and has volume behind it, then walk it end to end with somebody who does it.

> Point a model at a process that was never questioned and you get the same process, running faster.

1. **Pick a process with volume.** Customer onboarding, invoicing, quoting, weekly reporting. Something that happens every week and leaves a mark on the result.
2. **Watch where it slows down.** The handoffs, the retyping, the waiting, the correcting of somebody else's work. That is where the hours and the mistakes are.
3. **Test it against three filters.** Volume, checkability and cost. Volume means it happens often enough to matter. Checkability means a person can tell whether the output is good. Cost means there is a visible price today for doing it slowly or wrongly.
4. **Size it.** Multiply the frequency by the time each run takes. A small number means it stays on the list and waits its turn.
5. **Cut it to two or three.** Twenty use cases is a wish list. Two with an owner each is a plan.

Cutting the list is how the two that survive get the attention they need.

## Where the mapping stalls

**The list belongs to everybody.** Each team keeps its own version, nobody has said which two matter for the business, and the mapping ends as a document that nobody opens again.

**The data sits in the wrong place.** The use case looks clean on the process map. The customer history sits in a tool nobody wants to touch, the numbers arrive by email on Friday, and the pilot dies on data readiness before it ever reaches the people who would use it.

**The process itself is the problem.** Half of CEOs (50%) name AI technologies as the single largest contributor to productivity gains over the past 12 months, just ahead of business process redesign at 46%. ([EY-Parthenon CEO Outlook Survey](https://www.ey.com/en_gl/newsroom/2026/10/ai-drives-productivity-but-ceos-struggle-to-turn-gains-into-grow), October 2026) The two numbers sit close together, and the second one is the one companies skip. AI transformation is a change in how the work gets done. Point a model at a process that was never questioned and you get the same process, running faster.

## Making the first use case stick

The first use case is where the change is won or lost. Four things decide it.

<ul class="common-list">
  <li><strong>Start small.</strong> One use case, one team, a few weeks. Ship the small thing, learn, repeat.</li>
  <li><strong>Run it as an experiment.</strong> A hypothesis that can fail, a measure agreed before the start, and a date when you decide.</li>
  <li><strong>Set the goal in the language of the business.</strong> "The customer report goes out in two hours instead of six" is a goal. "We rolled out an AI assistant" is a project.</li>
  <li><strong>Decide where the freed hours go.</strong> A pilot that works and changes nothing is a costly demo.</li>
</ul>

The fourth one is the one teams skip. When the pilot works and the hours come back, where do the hours go? Left alone they fill up with the old way of working, because that is where the pressure already is. Somebody has to name the destination before the pilot starts.

Expect the emotional part, and give it room. The people closest to the work have the strongest view on whether the tool is any good, and they are usually right about the details. They are also the ones who decide quietly whether the new way really happens.

Their question is about their work, their standing and the colleagues they work with. Answer that before you ask them to change anything.

A pilot that ends without a decision teaches the company that AI was a waste of money. That lesson is expensive, and it is much harder to undo than a slow start.
