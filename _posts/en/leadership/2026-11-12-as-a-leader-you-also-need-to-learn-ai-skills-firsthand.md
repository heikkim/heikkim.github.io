## Your own AI skills matter too

*Eighty percent of CEOs say AI will change roles, skills and ways of working more than it will change headcount over the next three years. Only 15 percent of them say the productivity gains are being reinvested in workforce development.* ([EY-Parthenon CEO Outlook Survey](https://www.ey.com/en_gl/newsroom/2026/10/ai-drives-productivity-but-ceos-struggle-to-turn-gains-into-grow), October 2026)

The distance between those two numbers is a skills gap, and closing it starts with you.

> If you have told the team for two years that AI matters and never opened it yourself, you have **definitely** taught them something.

This is a leadership problem more than a technical one. The people with the most say in how the work gets done are also the ones with the least time to learn new tools. The rest of the company takes its cue from them, whether they mean to or not.

Most of what your company knows about AI today is what you and a handful of others learned last half a year ago. The tools move faster than that, and the new capability arrives quietly. A playbook written a year ago is more than out of date. **It is confidently wrong.**

## Nothing beats leading by example

The most effective way to build fluency inside a company is the leader using it in their own working day, in the open, on real work.

An executive who drafts with AI and prepares the board update with it is doing three things at once.
<ul class="success-list">
  <li>They get their own hours back.</li>
  <li>They show the team that this is the new normal.</li>
  <li>They generate tangible knowledge of AI benefits.</li>
</ul>

If you have told the team for two years that AI matters and never opened it yourself, you have **definitely** taught them something. People always follow their leader, for good and for worse.

An hour of hands-on work with your own real tasks teaches more than a day of slides, because you know immediately where AI helps and where it falls short. After you've become good in applying AI in your own work, you gather extremely helpful insights from AI:
<ul class="success-list">
  <li>Knowing when to trust it.</li>
  <li>Knowing when to challenge it.</li>
  <li>Knowing when it's you who's accountable for the result.</li>
</ul>

## The gap is about management

The tools work, and the managers who have tried them know it.

What goes wrong is what happens next. The tools recover lost time, which then gets absorbed into the old way of working. This happens because the question of what else it should go to **never gets asked**. The managers left to work this out alone get no guidance from the leadership and no examples from their peers, which is how a new habit dies quietly.

This is why a rollout stalls with nobody deciding to stop it. The tool was bought, the licence was paid for, the pilot worked. Opposition plays no part. There is just nobody in the company whose daily practice shows what to do with the extra hour recovered.

The solution is a leader, who is using the tools in the open, on real work. This way the leader becomes aware, firsthand, on the impact AI has on work and in the bigger picture the whole company. Driven by insight and knowledge, guiding people to the right direction becomes natural.

## Low-barrier entry to automation for leaders

The barrier to starting is the choice of the first task. A good first task is one you already do every week, one with an obvious right answer, and one small enough to check in a few minutes.

Three entry points to start already today:
1. Organising your inbox, so the messages that need you are handled first and the rest is summarised.
2. Drafting the responses, so you start from a reply instead of a blank page.
3. Preparing for meetings, so you walk in with the numbers and open questions ready.

Every one of them is ordinary, each takes just minutes to check, so the first attempt is small enough to learn from and to repeat. Judgement comes from practice, and the practice starts with a task you can finish today.

The skill you build this way is the one the company copies.
