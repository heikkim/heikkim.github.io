More than half of the companies I visit have an AI policy. It is a document that has been approved, circulated for comments and filed in a folder nobody opens.

The problem is rarely what the policy says. The problem is that it was written to the wrong place.

## Governance belongs to decisions, not to drawers

Governance means that somebody makes a decision and asks at the same time: is this allowed, is this risk accepted, and who is accountable if it goes wrong?

If nobody answers those questions, the company does not have AI governance. It has guidelines that nobody asks about.

In practice this comes down to a few things:

- Tools are approved on a named, maintained list.
- Data is shared only where it is attached to a case.
- Personal accounts never stay with an individual user.
- Every new use case goes through the same short check.

<!--content-image-->

## Short beats comprehensive

I always try to keep the governance policy under three pages. That is uncomfortable for governance that is written into drawers, but in practice three pages get circulated and three pages eventually get read.

A longer document is not more governable. It is just less read.

What I often see instead is a document with 12 pages of principles, 3 pages of roles and zero lines about who does what on Monday morning. That is the wrong order. Principles are easy to write and hard to live by. Roles and decision paths are what actually stop a bad decision.

Governance is not a documentation exercise. It is a way of making bad decisions less often.
